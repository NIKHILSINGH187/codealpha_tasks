01

What phishing actually is

Phishing is an attempt to trick someone into handing over information — a password, a card number, access to a system — by pretending to be someone trustworthy. It relies on impersonation and psychology, not on breaking encryption or exploiting software.

Email phishing

Mass emails impersonating a bank, vendor, or internal team, aimed at whoever happens to click.

Spear phishing

Targeted at one person, using real details about them (name, role, recent activity) to seem credible.

Whaling

Spear phishing aimed at executives or finance staff, often impersonating a CEO requesting a wire transfer.

Smishing / Vishing

The same tactic delivered by text message or phone call instead of email.

Clone phishing

A copy of a real, previously-sent email with the links or attachments swapped for malicious ones.

Angler phishing

Fake customer-support accounts on social media that "help" you by asking for login details.

02

Anatomy of a phishing email

Most phishing emails share the same handful of tells. Here's a typical one, annotated.

From"IT Support" <it-support@paypa1-security.com>
Toyou@yourcompany.com
SubjectURGENT: Your account will be suspended in 24 hours

Dear Valued Customer,

We have detected unusual activity on your account. You must verify your identity within 24 hours or your account will be permanently suspended.

Click the link below to confirm your details immediately:

http://paypal-account-verify.secure-loginhelp.com/confirm

Thank you for your promptly attention to this matter.

  • 1 Sender domain is misspelled ("paypa1" with the numeral 1) and unrelated to the real company.
  • 2 Generic greeting instead of your actual name — legitimate services you have an account with usually address you by name.
  • 3 Manufactured urgency and a threat ("24 hours," "suspended") designed to make you act before you think.
  • 4 The link's visible text doesn't match where it actually leads — hovering over it (without clicking) reveals the real destination.
  • 5 Awkward grammar ("promptly attention") — a small tell, but a real one, especially from a company that sends millions of emails.
03

The psychology behind it

Phishing works because it targets how people make decisions under pressure, not technical weaknesses. Attackers lean on a small set of psychological levers, usually more than one at once.

Urgency

"Act now or lose access" — pressure to skip verification and act on impulse.

Authority

Impersonating a boss, bank, or government agency so the request feels non-negotiable.

Fear

Threats of account suspension, fines, or legal trouble to provoke a panicked response.

Curiosity

Vague subject lines like "Invoice attached" or "You have a new voicemail" to get the click.

Trust

Spoofed sender names, logos, and formatting that mimic a real, familiar brand or coworker.

Reciprocity

Fake rewards, refunds, or gifts that create a sense of obligation to respond.

04

Spotting a fake website

Fake login pages are usually pixel-perfect copies of the real thing. The tell is almost never the design — it's the address bar.

  • → Check the domain carefully — micros0ft-login.com and login-microsft.net are not Microsoft.
  • → A padlock icon only means the connection is encrypted — it says nothing about who owns the site. Attackers get padlocks too.
  • → Type the address yourself or use a saved bookmark instead of clicking a link in an email or text.
  • → Be suspicious of login pages reached through a redirect, a QR code, or a shortened URL you can't preview.
  • → If a site asks for more than it should — a password and a card number and a security question — that's a red flag.
05

Best practices

  • 01 Pause before clicking anything urgent — attackers are counting on speed, not accuracy.
  • 02 Verify unusual requests (wire transfers, password resets, gift-card purchases) through a separate channel — call the person, don't reply to the email.
  • 03 Hover over links to preview the real URL before clicking.
  • 04 Enable multi-factor authentication everywhere it's offered, so a stolen password alone isn't enough.
  • 05 Never enter credentials on a page you reached by clicking a link — navigate there directly instead.
  • 06 Report suspicious emails to your IT/security team instead of just deleting them — one report can protect the whole organization.
  • 07 Keep software and browsers updated — some phishing pages exploit outdated software after the initial click.
06

Real-world patterns

Case pattern — Business Email Compromise

The fake CEO wire transfer

An attacker registers a domain one character off from a real company's, then emails someone in finance impersonating the CEO, urgently requesting a wire transfer while "in a meeting." These scams have collectively cost businesses billions of dollars and typically succeed by combining authority and urgency with a request that seems routine.

Case pattern — Credential harvesting worm

The fake shared document

A message claims a colleague shared a document and links to a login page that looks identical to a real file-sharing or email provider. Anyone who signs in hands over their credentials, and their account is then used to send the same message to their contacts — letting it spread on its own.

Case pattern — Delivery/package scam

The fake missed delivery

A text or email claims a package couldn't be delivered and asks for a small "redelivery fee," collecting card details on a convincing but fake courier website. It works because it references something plausible and mundane rather than anything that would raise suspicion.
